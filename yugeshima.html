01 PROJECT

Architecture as a living archive.

The Japan Studio research proposal explores the adaptive reuse of an akiya on Yugeshima, responding to depopulation and the post-growth condition of rural Japan.

The project investigates how an existing building can be structurally reinforced and adapted while retaining the history and memories embedded within its materials. Rather than treating the vacant house as an object to be restored to a fixed historical condition, the proposal considers the building as a living archive — continuously shaped by the people who inhabit, modify and interact with it.

The resulting creative residence provides a framework for artists, makers and designers to inhabit, work and exchange ideas within the existing structure.

02 RESEARCH HYPOTHESIS

RESEARCH HYPOTHESIS

A building and its materials hold and reflect the interactions that the space has had with people and its surroundings, telling a story of memory, programme and use.

The habits and routines of people have the ability to leave marks on a space — constantly evolving and being developed upon as users come and go.

04 RESEARCH

RESEARCH 01

Post Growth & Depopulation

人口減少とポスト成長

FIG. 02 — KAMIJIMA AGE / GENDER DATA
FIG. 03 — KAMIJIMA TOWN

Located in the Ehime region of Japan, the seaside town of Kamijima is facing depopulation and the effects of a post-growth society.

Younger generations commonly migrate towards major cities on the mainland in search of tertiary education and employment. This leaves many rural communities with ageing populations, vacant homes and underutilised spaces.

Rather than viewing these conditions as the end of a community, the project reframes population decline as an opportunity for renewal.

FIG. 04 — SHIMANAMI KAIDO

RESEARCH 02

Akiya

空き家

FIG. 05 — VACANT HOUSE ON YUGESHIMA

Akiya (空き家) are vacant homes that have become widespread throughout rural Japan as a result of depopulation and changing economic conditions.

These buildings represent more than vacant structures. They contain traces of previous inhabitation, material histories and cultural memory.

The project therefore approaches the akiya not as a building to erase and replace, but as an existing archive that can support new forms of inhabitation.

RESEARCH 03

The Shipbuilding Industry

造船業

FIG. 06 — HABU PORT
FIG. 07 — SHIPBUILDING / FERRY

Kamijima has a long-standing history of shipbuilding, shaped by its proximity to the Seto Inland Sea and its sheltered waters.

The proposal investigates the possibility of sourcing unused steel scaffolding from shipbuilding sites around Kamijima and reusing it as a flexible intervention within the creative residence.

The material therefore carries a connection to the industrial history of the island while providing a new function within the architecture.

RESEARCH 04

Japanese Construction Techniques

日本の建築技術

SASHIMONO
SHOJI / FUSUMA
ENGAWA / TATAMI

Traditional Japanese construction methods embody principles of craftsmanship, adaptability and respect for natural materials.

Sashimono (指物) allows timber elements to be assembled, disassembled and modified over time. Shoji (障子) and fusuma (襖) allow spaces to be divided, joined and reconfigured.

The concept of tatami (畳) and the measurement of rooms through the number of mats they accommodate informs the proposal's approach to programme. Rather than fixing each room to a single function, the building elements themselves define the potential uses of the space.

RESEARCH 05

Material History

材料の記憶

FIG. 08 — WEATHERED MATERIALS

Materials hold memory through the physical changes they exhibit. Rust, patina, stains, cracks and wear reveal the passage of time and the presence of people.

Timber darkens, warps and wears through repeated contact. Door handles, balustrades, stairs, tatami and sliding door tracks can become high-touch zones where human movement is recorded directly into the material.

These traces transform the building into a living archive, where material change becomes an expression of inhabitation.

RESEARCH 06

Preservation & Reinforcement

保存と補強

OLD

TIMBER

Human use, moisture, decay and environmental exposure leave physical traces within the existing structure.

NEW

STEEL

Steel provides structural strength, durability and a clear distinction between the existing and new.

FIG. 12 — STEEL / TIMBER JUNCTION

The proposal uses the contrasting ageing properties of timber and steel to express both memory and renewal.

Worn timber records the history of the akiya, while new steel provides reinforcement and a new structural language. Rather than hiding the intervention, the new material is deliberately expressed.

RESEARCH 07

Steel Reinforcement

FIG. 13 — STEEL CAPPING DETAIL
FIG. 14 — TIMBER / STEEL REINFORCEMENT

A range of steel sections can be used to reinforce aged timber columns and beams while retaining the original structural elements.

Equal angles, PFCs and universal sections can be introduced as new structural components, allowing the timber to remain visible and legible as part of the building's history.

RESEARCH 08

Ship of Theseus

テセウスの船

FIG. 15 — SHIP OF THESEUS DIAGRAM

The Ship of Theseus paradox questions whether an object remains the same when all of its parts are gradually replaced.

This provides a conceptual framework for the renovation of the akiya. Traditional Japanese buildings have historically allowed individual components to be repaired, replaced and renewed while retaining the identity of the building.

The project asks whether preservation can therefore occur through change rather than resisting it.

05 RESEARCH PROPOSAL

THE PROPOSAL

A creative residence for Yugeshima.

A framework for memory, reinforcement, adaptation and creative exchange.

01

Spatial Adaptability

空間の適応性

The creative residence is designed to support a range of creative residents including painters, sculptors, furniture designers, filmmakers, seamstresses and students.

Rather than assigning each room a fixed programme, adaptable architectural elements allow the spaces to change according to the needs of their occupants.

FIG. 16 — CREATIVE RESIDENTS

02

Timber Adaptability

FIG. 17 — SHOJI / FUSUMA ADAPTABILITY

The layering of fusuma and shoji enables the interior to shift between open and enclosed conditions.

Sliding or removing panels allows spaces to expand for collaboration or contract for individual work. The system responds to varying requirements for light, privacy, activity and season.

04

Temporary Scaffolding

一時的な足場

FIG. 18 — SCAFFOLDING INTERVENTION

The courtyard becomes a space for temporary construction. Reused steel scaffolding allows residents to customise their working environment according to their craft.

A painter may create a sheltered spray-painting area. A seamstress may use the structure to hang fabric. A carpenter may create additional storage. A short-term resident may simply use it to hang laundry.

The intervention is deliberately temporary, allowing the courtyard to continually change as different people occupy the residence.

dot architects — Kitakagaya, Osaka

Chidori Bunka transforms a former industrial building into an evolving community space. Salvaged materials are redistributed within the project and artists are encouraged to construct their own workspaces.

The project informs the proposal's approach to architecture as a framework for continuous adaptation.

FIG. 23 — HANAJU RENOVATION

Hanaju Renovation

MARU.architecture — Taito-ku, Tokyo

The 150-year-old flower shop combines preserved elements with a new steel structure.

The project demonstrates how old and new can be woven together rather than separated, creating a form of dynamic preservation.

10 CONCLUSION

The building does not need to remain unchanged to retain its memory.

Through selective reinforcement, material expression and adaptable interventions, Nagi's House becomes a framework for continued inhabitation.

The project proposes that preservation can occur through change — allowing the building's history to remain visible while new inhabitants add their own layer to its story.
