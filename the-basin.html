Design Studio D / Basin — 2025

Thresholds of Renewal in The Basin: Reconnecting The Basin's Landscape, Heritage, and Future

The Basin, Victoria, Australia

Project

Thresholds are not only indicators of space and programme, delineating characteristics of the two, but also connections between old and new.

Located within The Basin in the Dandenong Ranges, Intersection 3 was previously used as a rehabilitation centre for teenagers and young adults. The project reimagines the site as a public and social space for the wider Basin township, reconnecting its agricultural history, existing buildings and surrounding landscape.

The proposal establishes a series of thresholds between existing and proposed, public and agricultural, and built form and landscape. Small architectural interventions and linear elements create new relationships between spaces, allowing existing structures to be given renewed programmes rather than simply being replaced.

Hydrology became an integral part of this approach. The existing floodplain is treated as an asset and a part of the site's civic landscape, with gutters, downpipes, retaining walls and permeable surfaces forming a considered water management strategy across the site.

Hero Image

Overview

The project transforms Intersection 3 from a private rehabilitation ground into a multifunctional community centre. The proposal introduces a chapel, amphitheatre, cafe and gallery, educational spaces and a series of landscape interventions that reconnect the site with the wider Basin township.

Rather than treating the existing buildings as obstacles, the project uses adaptive reuse to establish new relationships between existing and proposed structures. Lightweight steel planes, concrete and masonry form a material language that responds to the site's agricultural and industrial character.

The project was developed through site analysis, precedent studies, drawing, physical model making and iterative testing of structural, material and spatial relationships.

Process

The design process began with an investigation into the existing conditions of Intersection 3, including its buildings, floodplain, agricultural areas, topography and hydrological infrastructure.

The concept of the threshold emerged through studies of existing and proposed conditions. Inspired by the work of Gordon Matta-Clark and Diller Scofidio + Renfro, subtraction, addition and the creation of voids became ways of questioning how existing structures could be given new life.

The work of Glenn Murcutt informed the project's approach to lightweight steel construction, structural expression and sensitivity to the Australian landscape.

Design

The final proposal establishes a sequence of civic and agricultural territories across the site. The Dairy Building becomes an important threshold between these two conditions, retaining its agricultural relationship while introducing public and social programmes.

Existing structures are connected to new lightweight interventions through a language of folded and bent steel planes. These elements wrap around buildings, form shelters, define circulation and create moments of occupation between old and new.

Water is incorporated into the architectural language through a site-wide gutter and downpipe strategy. Rather than treating water collection as secondary infrastructure, the system becomes an architectural threshold that connects roofs, buildings and landscape.

Drawings

The project was resolved through a series of site plans, building plans, sections, axonometrics and detail studies. These drawings investigate the relationship between the existing buildings, new interventions, landscape and hydrological systems.

The Utility Station became a key architectural study, demonstrating how a singular linear threshold can connect an existing shed to a new programmed workshop while also collecting and directing water.

Material

Materiality was developed in response to both the existing character of Intersection 3 and the environmental conditions of The Basin. Concrete blocks and masonry establish a robust and durable base, while lightweight steel interventions distinguish new thresholds from the existing structures.

The contrast between heavy existing materials and lightweight steel creates a clear distinction between old and new while allowing the two conditions to remain visually connected.

Physical model making was particularly important in testing this material relationship. Liquid copper paint and patina were used to represent water movement across the models, making hydrology visible as part of the architectural expression.

Hydrology

The Basin's floodplain became an important design condition rather than a constraint to be hidden. The proposal uses the existing landscape to direct and store stormwater, allowing the floodplain to function as a natural part of the site's water management system.

Buildings are elevated where appropriate, while retaining walls, permeable surfaces and landscape interventions guide water towards the floodplain. The architectural gutter system provides a consistent connection between roof, structure and landscape.

Hydrology / Landscape Drawing

Final Design

The completed design establishes a civic intervention that reconnects the landscape, heritage and future of The Basin. The programme is distributed across the site to create a sequence of social, cultural, spiritual and agricultural spaces.

The chapel provides a space for reflection, while the amphitheatre creates a flexible setting for performances, talks and community events. The cafe and gallery activate the existing Dairy Building, creating a threshold between agricultural and social territories.

Across the site, the architecture works as a series of connected thresholds rather than independent objects. A sink becomes a bench, a workshop becomes a shelter, and a gutter becomes a connection between existing and proposed.

Model

Physical models were used throughout the project to test the relationship between landscape, structure, material and water. Models were produced at multiple scales, from 1:1500 and 1:1000 site models to detailed 1:25 studies of the Utility Station.

The models allowed the relationship between the existing and proposed conditions to be understood spatially, while making the project's material and hydrological language tangible.
