Landscape as an Archive

Housing as Spatial Assemblage

Ocean Grove, Victoria, Australia

PROJECT

Ocean Grove is experiencing increasing demand for housing, driven by the appeal of coastal, slower-paced living. At the expanding suburban edge of Kingston Coast, this demand is accompanied by the levelling of landscape, removal of native vegetation and repetition of standardised housing models.

Landscape as an Archive: Housing as Spatial Assemblage explores an alternative approach to housing — one that accommodates density while retaining the spatial and material memory of the ground.

Rather than treating housing as a finished object, the project proposes built form as an incremental framework. Topography, vegetation and existing landscape conditions become active generators of the architecture, while adaptable spaces allow patterns of inhabitation to emerge over time.

RESEARCH QUESTION

In an environment where housing needs are increasing, but are damaging to the ground condition, how can built form be conceived as a framework that accommodates this demand while sustaining the spatial and material memory of the landscape, in turn allowing patterns of inhabitation to be expressed through form over time?

01 / LANDSCAPE AS ARCHIVE

The landscape of Ocean Grove is defined by a fragile coastal ecology of sand dunes, low coastal scrub, wetlands and wind-shaped topographies.

Contemporary housing development often disrupts this relationship through extensive site levelling, removal of vegetation and the production of uniform lots. The landscape becomes a neutralised surface rather than an active ecological and cultural substrate.

The project instead considers the landscape as an archive: a record of spatial, material and ecological conditions that can inform how housing is positioned and inhabited.

02 / THE SITE

The project is located within Lots 607–621 of the Kingston Coast Stage 6 development in Ocean Grove. Positioned between Coastal Boulevard and Duneview Drive, the site sits at the edge of suburban development and expansive farmland.

This edge condition creates an opportunity to reconsider the relationship between housing, landscape and ecological systems. Wildlife corridors, productive landscapes, water-sensitive urban design and gradual density become generators for the proposal.

03 / SPATIAL ASSEMBLAGE

The project draws on ideas of Supports and Infill, Shearing Layers of Change and Aldo van Eyck's conception of the in-between.

Housing is understood as a framework rather than a completed object. Slower elements such as site and structure establish permanence, while faster layers remain capable of adaptation as residents and their needs change.

Thresholds become important spaces within this framework. Rather than treating circulation as purely functional, decks, courtyards and shared spaces create opportunities for interaction between dwelling, landscape and community.

04 / THE PROPOSAL

The proposal develops a cluster housing model that concentrates built form while preserving landscape between and around the dwellings.

Internal corridors are reconsidered and moved outward, creating a network of decks, courtyards and shared thresholds. These spaces become both circulation and places for gathering.

Productive gardens and semi-transparent greenhouse spaces introduce another layer of shared occupation, allowing residents to participate in the landscape beyond the boundaries of their individual dwelling.

05 / TYPOLOGIES

The dwelling typologies follow a growth and division strategy. A wall extending beyond the envelope of the residence separates living and sleeping programmes, allowing the dwelling to expand as additional bedrooms are required.

This creates a framework capable of accommodating different family sizes and patterns of inhabitation while maintaining a consistent architectural language.

06 / MODEL STUDIES

Physical models were used to investigate the relationship between dwelling, threshold and landscape, and to explore how different typologies could be assembled within the cluster.
