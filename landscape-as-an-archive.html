Landscape as an Archive: Housing as Spatial Assemblage

Ocean Grove, Victoria, Australia

PROJECT

Ocean Grove is experiencing an increased demand for housing due to the appeal of coastal, slower-paced living. This demand is supported by the availability of land, particularly within expanding suburban edges such as Kingston Coast, resulting in widespread levelling of the landscape, removal of native vegetation, and the production of repetitive housing models that disregard ground conditions.

Built form can instead be conceived as an incremental spatial framework that accommodates density while sustaining the spatial and material memory embedded within the landscape.

By first responding to existing topography, ground conditions and flora, this thesis presents housing as an adaptable model rather than a finished object. Patterns of inhabitation are encouraged to become legible in form, allowing dwelling to evolve alongside the ground rather than overwrite it.

RESEARCH QUESTION

In an environment where housing needs are increasing, but are damaging to the ground condition, how can built form be conceived as a framework that accommodates this demand while sustaining the spatial and material memory of the landscape, in turn allowing patterns of inhabitation to be expressed through form over time?

THE SITE

The project is located within Kingston Coast Stage 6 in Ocean Grove on the Bellarine Peninsula. Lots 607 to 621 form a medium-density suburban grain positioned between Coastal Boulevard and Duneview Drive.

The site sits at the edge of the housing development, creating a condition between suburban development and productive farmland.

This edge condition raises questions of wildlife and green corridors, productive landscapes, water-sensitive urban design, gradual density and the relationship between housing and the landscape.

LANDSCAPE CONDITIONS

Ocean Grove's landscape condition is defined by a fragile coastal ecology of sand dunes, low coastal scrub, wetlands and wind-shaped topographies.

Contemporary housing developments disrupt this relationship through extensive site levelling, removal of native vegetation and the flattening of landscape to produce uniform lots.

The project instead asks how housing can respond to these existing conditions and allow the landscape to remain an active participant in the formation of architecture.

CONCEPT

Rock formations along the Surf Coast became an early point of departure for the project. Through erosion and subtraction, natural elements create cavities, spaces and places within the rock.

The project explores a similar process within built form — using subtraction, thresholds and interstitial spaces to create places that can be occupied, adapted and transformed by residents.

RESEARCH

The project draws on theories of Supports and Infill by N. John Habraken, Stewart Brand's Shearing Layers of Change, and Aldo van Eyck's ideas of interstitial and in-between spaces.

Together these ideas position housing as an open-ended framework rather than a fixed object, allowing occupation, space and material conditions to change over time.

PRECEDENTS

Selected precedents include Diagoon Housing by Herman Hertzberger, the Amsterdam Orphanage by Aldo van Eyck, IBAVI 9 Social Housing Units in Inca, Ocean Grove House by Kennedy Nolan, The Paddock by CROSBY Architects, and Bluefield Housing by Damien Madigan.

These projects informed the development of adaptable housing, cluster arrangements, thresholds, permeability, shared spaces and relationships between dwelling and landscape.

PROPOSAL

Housing is conceived as a spatial framework rather than a finished object.

The proposal develops cluster housing around shared landscape, productive gardens and outdoor circulation. By removing the conventional internal corridor, circulation becomes an opportunity for interaction through shared external spaces.

The resulting arrangement creates a gradient between private, communal and landscape spaces, allowing patterns of inhabitation to develop over time.

HOUSING TYPOLOGIES

The housing typologies follow a strategy of growth and division. A plane extends beyond the envelope of the dwelling, separating living and sleeping spaces while allowing the house to expand as additional bedrooms are required.

The system allows different household configurations to occupy variations of the same underlying framework.
