Converging Communities: Reimagining Sunbury

Sunbury, Victoria, Australia

Project

Converging Communities explores how housing can become a framework for living with Country rather than a system that separates people from the landscape. The project questions conventional housing typologies and the fragmented landscapes produced through colonisation and urbanisation.

Located in Sunbury on Wurundjeri Country, the proposal responds to a site shaped by agricultural histories, Indigenous cultural significance, urban expansion and ecological degradation. Rather than treating the site as vacant land available for development, the project considers it as a layered landscape with histories, relationships and ecological systems that should continue to inform its future.

The proposal brings together housing, productive landscapes, biodiversity-sensitive urban design, water-sensitive urban design and shared community spaces. By concentrating housing into a connected cluster, more of the existing landscape can remain open, allowing ecological systems and community life to overlap.

The project asks how architecture can contribute to reconciliation with Country while supporting a growing community. Rather than attempting to recreate a pre-colonial landscape, the proposal seeks to work with Country through regeneration, reciprocity and an acknowledgement of the site's continuing cultural and ecological significance.

Positioning

The project began with an investigation into my own positionality as a designer. As a second-generation immigrant with Singaporean, Hokkien and Chinese heritage, my understanding of place has been shaped by multiple cultural contexts, values and systems of knowledge.

This positioning became an important part of understanding how architectural education and Western design practices can privilege particular ways of seeing, mapping and understanding land.

The project therefore asks the designer to move from an ego-centric to an eco-centric position, recognising that Country encompasses interconnected human and non-human systems rather than simply functioning as a site for development.

Positioning / Personal Timeline

Country

Understanding Country became central to the project. Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung Country is not understood simply as a physical site or parcel of land, but as a holistic system encompassing people, plants, animals, soil, water, sky, geology, atmosphere, culture and spirit.

This differs significantly from the Western conception of the site, where land can be measured, divided, owned and developed. The project instead considers the relationships between these systems and asks how architecture can operate within them without reinforcing their separation.

The research into Biik Wurrdha, Jacksons Creek, and the surrounding landscape revealed the importance of water, vegetation, geology and movement in understanding the site. These relationships informed the ecological and spatial strategies developed throughout the proposal.

Country / Site Analysis

Site

Sunbury's landscape has been significantly transformed through colonisation, agriculture and urban expansion. The site contains remnants of ecological systems alongside evidence of development and degradation.

Site investigations considered the existing vegetation, waterways, geology, topography, biodiversity, heritage zones and surrounding development. Species including red gum, kangaroo grass, blackwood and common reed helped reveal the ecological character of the landscape.

The site also sits within a landscape containing significant Indigenous heritage. Sunbury's earth rings and surrounding cultural heritage demonstrate that the landscape cannot be understood independently from its history and continuing cultural significance.

These conditions became generators for the design rather than constraints to be overcome.

Biodiversity

Biodiversity-Sensitive Urban Design became a key framework for reconsidering the relationship between housing and landscape. Green space alone does not necessarily provide biodiversity; the design must support habitat, movement, ecological processes and positive human-nature interaction.

The proposal therefore maintains and creates habitat, facilitates dispersal, supports natural processes and minimises anthropogenic threats. Existing ecological conditions are extended into the residential landscape so that biodiversity is not isolated from everyday life.

Water-sensitive urban design is integrated with these strategies through swales, wetlands and productive landscapes. Water becomes an ecological connector between the built environment and Country.

Biodiversity / WSUD Strategy

Engagement

Meaningful engagement with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples is essential when designing on Country. The project recognises that Indigenous Knowledge is particular to people and place and should not be extracted, generalised or represented without appropriate permission.

Engagement requires listening, consultation, consent, attribution, reciprocity and respect for Indigenous agency. A community cannot be represented by a single voice, and cultural knowledge should only be incorporated through appropriate relationships with Traditional Custodians and knowledge holders.

For the project, this meant understanding the importance of Registered Aboriginal Parties, cultural heritage processes and the need to engage with Traditional Owners and heritage advisors throughout the design process.

Precedents

Precedent studies investigated ways of creating environmentally regenerative and socially connected communities while challenging conventional housing models.

Dairy Road demonstrated how a site can become a resource, using localised skills and materials, circular production, regenerative systems and renewed biodiversity.

Glenard Estate by Walter Burley Griffin demonstrated a sensitivity to contours and indigenous vegetation, while Witchcliffe Ecovillage explored shared resources, renewable energy, rainwater collection and sustainable materials.

Studio BELEM informed the use of modular systems and adaptable housing, while Diagoon Housing by Herman Hertzberger provided an important conceptual reference for understanding form as an instrument that residents can interpret and inhabit in different ways.

Manifesto

In architecture, the concept of deatomisation refers to the disassembly of structures or materials. This project challenges typical housing typologies and developments and advocates for a shift in design thinking and processes, challenging the paradigm of atomised materials and fragmented landscapes that has formed the basis of design thinking since colonisation.

By removing boundaries and rigidity between materials and instead celebrating biomimicry, holistic structures and spaces can be created that more intentionally resonate with their surroundings.

Through regenerative landscaping and community-centred design, the project aims to heal damage inflicted on the land through colonisation and urbanisation. Deatomisation minimises waste and negative environmental impact by promoting circular economy principles and materials that can be separated, reused and recycled at the end of a building's life cycle.

The project works toward an architecture that respects diversity, builds resilience and acknowledges our interconnectedness with Country — crafting a future where places of residence stand as a testament to our commitment to the planet and future generations.

Process

The design process moved iteratively between landscape, masterplanning, housing typology and material studies. Rather than establishing the building form first and fitting landscape around it, the proposal developed through an understanding of the ecological and social systems of the site.

The masterplan was repeatedly tested to determine how housing could be concentrated while maintaining landscape, habitat and shared community spaces.

Cluster housing became the preferred strategy because it allows multiple dwellings to share infrastructure and landscape while creating opportunities for interaction between residents.

Masterplan

The final masterplan establishes a clustered residential community embedded within a regenerative landscape. Buildings are positioned to create shared spaces while allowing ecological corridors to pass through and around the development.

Rather than extending a conventional suburban road and lot arrangement across the site, the masterplan prioritises landscape continuity, shared resources and pedestrian movement.

Productive gardens, shared open spaces and ecological corridors become part of the everyday experience of the residents. The resulting community is not organised as a collection of isolated houses, but as a network of relationships between people, buildings and Country.

Final Masterplan

Housing

The housing system challenges the idea that a dwelling should have a fixed programme and permanent configuration. Instead, the building acts as a framework that can adapt to different residents and changing demographics.

Horizontal walls are non-load-bearing, allowing rooms to be added or removed as required. Units can therefore expand or contract over time as households change.

Balconies and winter gardens provide additional spaces that can temporarily become part of the dwelling, extending habitable space beyond a fixed internal boundary.

This adaptability allows the architecture to accommodate different patterns of inhabitation rather than prescribing a single way of living.

Housing Typology / Adaptability

Community

Community is supported through a series of shared thresholds between private dwellings and the broader landscape. Outdoor decks, gardens and communal spaces provide opportunities for residents to encounter one another without forcing social interaction.

The cluster arrangement creates a shared centre that acts as an informal social space. Circulation is therefore shifted from an internal corridor to a network of outdoor thresholds and shared landscapes.

Productive gardens and communal spaces provide additional opportunities for residents to grow food, gather, work and participate in the stewardship of the landscape.

Third Spaces

Between the private dwelling and the wider community, third spaces provide places for informal occupation, gardening and gathering.

Inspired by productive gardens and semi-transparent structures, these spaces combine deck, shelter and landscape. They can function as greenhouses, communal gardens, outdoor rooms or places for residents to meet.

These spaces are intentionally open-ended. Their value is not determined by a single programme but by the ability of residents to appropriate them and give them meaning over time.

Third Space / Productive Garden

Material

Material selection responds to the project's emphasis on circularity, adaptability and connection to Country. Engineered timber forms the primary internal structure, including LVL columns and beams and CLT floor panels.

Lightweight steel is used for bracing and external connections, while rammed earth and polycarbonate contribute to the material character of the shared spaces.

Materials are considered as systems rather than isolated finishes. The structure is designed so that components can be separated and adapted, supporting the project's broader ambition to reduce waste and extend the useful life of the building.

Resolution

The material study resolves the relationship between engineered timber, lightweight steel, rammed earth and polycarbonate. The structural system establishes a clear framework within which rooms, thresholds and landscape spaces can change over time.

This approach supports the project's central idea that housing should not be conceived as a finished object. Instead, it becomes an adaptable framework that can accommodate changing patterns of inhabitation.

Care for Country

Care for Country is embedded within the project through ecological regeneration, biodiversity-sensitive design, water management and an understanding of cultural relationships to land.

Cultural burning was investigated as an example of how Indigenous knowledge can inform contemporary approaches to landscape management. Fire is not simply understood as a destructive force, but as a tool that can contribute to ecological health when undertaken according to the appropriate cultural knowledge and lore.

The proposal therefore seeks to support healthy Country rather than simply minimising environmental impact. The landscape becomes an active participant in the architecture, with biodiversity, water, vegetation and human occupation working together as an interconnected system.

Care for Country / Landscape Strategy

Model

Physical model making was used as an iterative design tool throughout the project. A 1:10,000 site model was developed to understand the relationship between the proposed buildings, topography and broader landscape.

The model developed through a process of constructing the landscape, placing building pieces, testing arrangements and refining the relationship between built form and open space.

Model making allowed the project to be understood as a landscape system rather than simply as a collection of buildings.

Final Design

Converging Communities proposes an alternative model for residential development in which density does not require the complete occupation of the ground.

By clustering housing, maintaining ecological corridors, integrating productive landscapes and creating shared thresholds, the proposal allows people to live within a landscape that continues to evolve.

The architecture is deliberately adaptable. Rooms can change, shared spaces can be appropriated and landscape can regenerate. Over time, the patterns of inhabitation become part of the character and memory of the place.

The project ultimately asks how architecture can move away from fragmented, rigid and extractive models of development toward a more relational approach — one that allows community, ecology and Country to converge.
